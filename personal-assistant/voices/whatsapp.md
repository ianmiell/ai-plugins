# Ian's WhatsApp voice

Read `common.md` first, then this, before writing any WhatsApp draft. Built
in October 2026 from about 12,000 of Ian's own WhatsApp messages over two
years: family 1:1s and the family group (about half), friends and old
colleagues (about a third), work contacts and clients who use WhatsApp
(about a tenth), and local, hobby and friends' groups.

WhatsApp is the most casual place Ian writes. It's Slack with the work
removed: texting while doing something else. A draft should look like
something typed on a phone with one thumb.

## Common rules

- **Very short.** The median message is five words; nine in ten are under
  fifteen. "ok", "yup", "yeah", "no idea!", "ta", "pls", "K", "cool",
  "pencilled in", "sounds good". Answer the question and stop.
- **Several messages, not one paragraph.** He sends 2–4 short lines in a
  row rather than one long message. A draft is one message, so write it as
  1–3 short lines with a line break where he'd hit send.
- **No greeting, no sign-off, never "Ian".** The exception: "hey [name]" or
  "Hey [nickname]" to restart a chat that's been quiet for weeks.
- **Lower case is normal.** About 40% of messages start lower case,
  including "i" in quick replies ("i could take holiday that week"). Longer
  or more careful messages, and the first message to someone he doesn't
  know well, are sentence case. Don't mix the two in one message.
- **Almost no full stops** at the end of a line (under one in ten). A
  question mark when it's a question; a lone "?" to chase a reply.
- **Reactions and acknowledgements:** "lol", "haha", "IKR?", "wise",
  "ah", "aye", "cool". Often the whole reply.
- **Emoji are rare** (about one message in a hundred) and small: 🙂 😉 😀
  🙁 👍 ✅ 😆. At most one, usually at the end of a dry line ("Depends 😀").
  Never hearts or strings of emoji.
- **"x" at the end** only with family and a few close friends, and only
  when the moment is warm ("Thanks mate x"). Never to work contacts.
- **Dashes are rare.** Commas and line breaks do the work. If one is
  needed, a spaced hyphen " - ", never an em dash.
- **Links go on their own**, with at most a short line before or after
  ("I'd like to see this, Sunday 12:45?").

## By audience

### Family

Logistics in the fewest words: what, when, who. "home about 6.45",
"leaving soon", "delivery due today", "receipt for the conference pls",
"is the 14th our weekend away?". Questions are direct and practical. Teasing
and dry asides are fine; nothing formal, ever. In the family group, keep it
to one line that everyone can answer ("anyone want cinema ticket codes?").

### Friends and old colleagues

Banter and plans. Matches the other person's register: if they swear or
mock, he gives it back drily ("probably true", "why don't you do it
then?"). Making plans: suggest one concrete option ("could do, or i could
come to you?"), then confirm in a word ("pencilled in", "see you there?").
Honest and brief about how he is, without detail.

### Work contacts on WhatsApp

Clients, partners, his CEO and old colleagues who message him here. Still
WhatsApp short, but sentence case is more common and he's careful about
facts: answer exactly what was asked ("no, but i think we're in the box
seat"). Humour stays dry and light ("Team is available at a reasonable rate
😆"). Anything that needs a proper answer, a document or a decision moves
to email or a call: "will send you a proper note tomorrow".

### Groups

Ian rarely posts in big groups. When he does, it's one line with a reason
to reply. Don't draft replies to neighbourhood or community groups unless
Ian asks; offer to do nothing.

## Don't

- Start with "Hi [name]," or end with a sign-off. Ever.
- Write a paragraph. If it needs more than three lines, suggest a call or
  an email instead.
- Use email or LinkedIn phrasing ("Thanks for getting in touch", "Let me
  know your thoughts").
- Add emoji to look friendly, or more than one.
- Copy someone else's register into a message to a third person (eg a
  friend's swearing into a family chat).
- Mention anything private from one chat in another.
